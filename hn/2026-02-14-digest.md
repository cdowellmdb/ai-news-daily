### [GPT-5.2 derives a new result in theoretical physics](https://openai.com/index/new-result-theoretical-physics/)
**Categories:** LLMs
**HN Link:** https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=47006594
**Score:** 536 | **Comments:** 365

#### Article Summary
OpenAI’s post summarizes a new preprint on scattering amplitudes in high-energy physics and highlights the role of GPT‑5.2 in conjecturing a compact formula. The preprint, titled “Single-minus gluon tree amplitudes are nonzero,” is authored by Alfredo Guevara, Alex Lupsasca, David Skinner, Andrew Strominger, and Kevin Weil, and focuses on gluons and their tree-level scattering amplitudes. The article explains that a scattering amplitude is the quantity used to compute the probability of specific particle interactions, and that for gluons many tree-level amplitudes simplify in unexpected ways. A long-standing textbook conclusion is that amplitudes with one negative-helicity gluon and the remaining gluons of positive helicity must vanish. The preprint argues that this conclusion is too strong because it assumes generic momenta. The authors identify a specific “half-collinear” slice of momentum space, a mathematically well-defined alignment of momenta, where that assumption fails and the amplitude is nonzero. The article emphasizes that the result is specific to this special kinematic regime rather than generic momenta, and positions it as a new opening for further theory development.

The post also lays out the methodology in detail. The human authors first computed explicit expressions for integer n up to n = 6, but those expressions are extremely complex and correspond to a Feynman-diagram expansion whose complexity grows superexponentially in n. GPT‑5.2 Pro then simplified those expressions into much more compact forms for the computed cases. From these simplified base cases, GPT‑5.2 Pro conjectured a closed-form formula intended to hold for all n. An internal scaffolded version of GPT‑5.2 then spent about 12 hours deriving a formal proof of the same formula. The resulting expression (Eq. 39 in the preprint) was checked by the authors in two standard ways: it satisfies the Berends–Giele recursion relation for building multi-particle tree amplitudes, and it is consistent with the soft theorem that constrains how amplitudes behave as a particle becomes soft. These checks are presented as the basis for confidence in the conjectured closed form.

The article stresses why this matters. In this area of physics, complicated textbook expressions often hide deeper structures, and a simple closed-form result can reveal new conceptual symmetry or organizing principles. The single-minus case had been largely set aside as “zero,” so a controlled regime in which it is nonzero reopens a line of inquiry. The post notes that, with GPT‑5.2’s help, the amplitude results have already been extended from gluons to gravitons, and that additional generalizations are in progress. It frames the work as an example of AI-assisted scientific discovery that couples domain-expert reasoning with LLM pattern recognition and formal verification steps. The article also highlights that the preprint is on arXiv, submitted for publication, and explicitly invites community feedback, signaling that the result is new and still under external scrutiny.

Limitations and open questions are acknowledged implicitly rather than spelled out as a list: the nonzero amplitude depends on a specific half-collinear regime rather than generic kinematics, and the broader implications are left to future work. The article suggests follow-up directions such as analogous amplitudes for gravitons and other generalizations, but does not provide performance benchmarks or empirical tests beyond analytic checks. Overall, the piece presents a concrete technical result (a closed-form amplitude in a special regime) and a methodology where GPT‑5.2 acts as a simplifier and conjecture generator, with humans and internal tooling supplying the proof and consistency checks.

### [gRPC deep dive: from service definition to wire format](https://kreya.app/blog/grpc-deep-dive/)
**Categories:** Coding Improvements
**HN Link:** https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=46944430
**Score:** 130 | **Comments:** 20

#### Article Summary
This Kreya blog post provides a layered explanation of how gRPC works, moving from high-level service definitions down to the HTTP/2 wire format. It begins by framing gRPC as a contract-first system built on Protocol Buffers. The `.proto` file defines message schemas and service methods, and `protoc` generates client stubs and server scaffolding across multiple languages. The post emphasizes that this contract is the source of truth, which prevents client/server drift and standardizes API shapes through method names like `/{Package}.{Service}/{Method}`. It then walks through gRPC’s four streaming models: unary calls, server streaming, client streaming, and bidirectional streaming. The author stresses that streaming is a first-class semantic in gRPC, not a bolted-on transport trick.

The article covers metadata, explaining that gRPC uses key-value pairs similar to HTTP headers and that keys ending with `-bin` represent binary metadata. It notes that metadata can be sent by both client and server, as headers at the start of a call or trailers at the end. This leads into the HTTP/2 transport layer explanation. Each gRPC call maps to a single HTTP/2 stream, allowing multiplexing of many calls over one TCP connection. The post notes that HTTP/2 solves head-of-line blocking at the request level, though TCP-level blocking still exists when packets are lost. This establishes the framing for how requests and responses are structured in HTTP/2 frames.

At the byte-level, the post explains gRPC’s message framing: each protobuf payload is wrapped in a 5-byte header consisting of a compression flag and a 4-byte big-endian message length. The author provides a worked example: a protobuf-encoded “fruit” message of 10 bytes is prefixed with a zero compression flag and a length of 0x0A, yielding a 15-byte message. This framing allows receivers to read each message boundary cleanly, which is especially important in streaming contexts. The post explains that metadata maps directly to HTTP/2 headers, while gRPC status and error information is delivered via trailers. The HTTP status code is usually 200 even on application errors; the real status is in `grpc-status` and `grpc-message`, and richer structured errors can be transmitted using `google.rpc.Status` serialized into the `grpc-status-details-bin` trailer.

Compression is addressed as an optional feature. The client advertises supported algorithms via `grpc-accept-encoding`, the server selects one with `grpc-encoding`, and the compression flag in the 5-byte header indicates whether each message is compressed. The author notes that compression can be asymmetric (different for request and response) and that overhead can outweigh benefits for very small messages. The post then discusses alternative transports such as Unix domain sockets and named pipes, positioning gRPC as a protocol that can be used for local IPC as well as networked services.

Finally, the article describes the “browser gap.” Browsers do not expose the low-level HTTP/2 features needed for gRPC, especially access to trailers and fine-grained stream control. gRPC-Web is presented as a workaround that encodes trailers into the data stream and supports text-based encodings so that browsers can participate. The conclusion reiterates that understanding the full stack—from contract to wire bytes—helps developers debug, design, and operate gRPC systems, even if tools like Kreya abstract most details away. The post is primarily educational and does not include benchmarks, but it provides concrete protocol details and emphasizes how each layer maps to the next.

The comment thread is small but candid. The dominant sentiment is that gRPC’s elegance on paper does not always translate to an easy developer experience, especially outside Go. One commenter recounts using gRPC in Rust and Python and encountering rough edges: opaque configuration, awkward error handling, and operational friction such as health checks through HAProxy. ### [How we interfaced single-threaded C++ with multi-threaded Rust](https://antithesis.com/blog/2026/rust_cpp/)
**Categories:** Coding Improvements
**HN Link:** https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=46923825
**Score:** 103 | **Comments:** 9

#### Article Summary
This Antithesis blog post explains how the company bridged a single-threaded C++ fuzzer with a multi-threaded, async Rust controller inside its deterministic testing platform. Antithesis runs customer software inside a deterministic hypervisor (“Determinator”) that replaces nondeterministic operations with controlled signals, making executions repeatable. A fuzzer generates control signals to drive the Determinator, and the fuzzer’s “controller” decides where to start and which inputs to supply. The core fuzzer is single-threaded C++, with a callback-style interface: `poll_for_inputs` returns a starting state and input bytes, and `advertise_outputs` sends observed outputs (logs, assertions, utilization) back to the controller. To experiment with new strategies, the team added a Rust interface, with the Rust side designed to be multi-threaded and async.

The post focuses on how to reconcile these two worlds: synchronous C++ and asynchronous Rust. The Rust side uses the `cxx` crate to generate an FFI boundary. This exposes Rust types to C++, C++ types to Rust, and shared plain-data structs that can cross the boundary. On the C++ side, the team wraps functions using the pimpl idiom to avoid recompiling large portions of code when Rust changes. For the sync/async mismatch, the design introduces async channels on the Rust side. The async controller runs on a runtime and communicates through channels to a synchronous Rust “adapter” that is called from C++. The synchronous adapter can poll channels without blocking, translating between C++ data structures and Rust data structures as it sends and receives messages.

The hard part is thread safety of C++ objects. The post recounts a failure: the author naively implemented `Send` for a C++ `State` type, which allowed it to cross thread boundaries. This caused intermittent segmentation faults because the C++ type internally used a non-thread-safe reference-counted pointer. By letting the object be cloned or dropped on a different thread, the refcount could race and free the object prematurely. The post explains Rust’s `Send` and `Sync` traits and why the compiler couldn’t automatically reason about C++ types, leading to the unsafe manual implementation and the resulting bug.

The fix is an ownership/borrowing pattern that preserves thread safety. The team created `CppOwner`, which owns a C++ object and must live on the main thread, and `CppBorrower`, which is a reference-like handle that can be passed across threads. Both wrap a Rust `Arc<T>` to track references, but only `CppOwner` is allowed to drop the underlying object. `CppBorrower` can be cloned and sent across threads, while `CppOwner` tracks “in flight” objects on the main thread and only drops them after all borrowers are gone. This design keeps refcount mutations on the main thread while allowing cross-thread access for reading and processing, avoiding races and segfaults. The post notes that this approach worked for about two years in production.
